# Personal Notes

## Age, Time, and Strong Opinions

I have strong opinions on code. Obviously.

When I actually got a job writing code back in 1999, over the next couple years I was really hot on adopting new technologies and agile practices that threw out the old ways of waterfall design, binders of requirements, monolithic inflexible systems, archaic languages. I was part of that Hackers generation - the first generation of kids that grew up with computers at home. I liked the feeling of rebellion. I felt irreverent. It was rock and roll.

I remember talking to an old COBOL engineer. They were lamenting how no one appreciated their skills, these new software practices and languages didn't understand what it takes to build a professional system. Kids these days.

Now I'm the old one.

The problem with having strong opinions and that feeling of irreverence - have I become the old COBOL engineer? Am I blind to the novel machinations of kids these days?

## Software Engineering in 2026 is Dead

Perhaps my fear is that software engineering - my entire career, something I've loved doing - is _dead_. There is real evidence it is. All my experiences, memories, skills, stories, jokes... all scraped into the countless anonymous matrices of LLMs and forgotten.

I could whinge: woe is me. AI can't write code better than I can. I could resist: I could be John Henry, code drivin' man, laying down byte after byte against the AI. Even if I won, I'd die. I could retire... well, no I couldn't. Who can afford that?

## Art and Seeing

Maybe the most fundamental thing art has taught me is _to see_. To see things for what they are, not interpreted, not as I expect them to look, not as society says I should see them. To recognize preconceptions and biases and expectations, set them aside, look again, repeat until what I'm looking at seems foreign and strange.

Once you can look at the world this way, you can accept the world as it is, not how you want or expect it to be.

## AI

AI has radically redefined my industry. Over the last couple years, it has rendered my skills at writing code irrelevant. It is quite amazing to bear witness to the death of an entire industry - one that had redefined culture, society, and the entire economy.

That may have residual detrimental effects on an old software engineer's brittle psyche. Especially one whose identity was so intimately tied to _code_. Bad recipe for a culture steeped in anxiety and depression and capitalism. Sink or swim. Your failures are your own, old man. 

I've had a good chunk of therapy, luckily. And have superb coworkers and comrades and friends. And an artist. Instead of indulging my insecurities and dismissing AI, I leaned into my curiosity. The AI world is absolutely chuck-full of hype and jargon. What could it actually do in the hands of someone who cared about the craft?

## Old Problems, New Solutions

When I worked at a huge corporate giant with hundreds of dev teams, I saw a lot of boilerplate developers. Teams would be given some problem, and the same pattern would be applied, the same framework used, literally the same code written and rewritten again and again. REST Controller, one line Service, ORM-managed queries. Things took weeks to deliver because every step in the process was rigid, standardized, form-driven. There was no creativity. It wasn't just the paperwork and bureaucracy. The culture was ingrained into the developers and managers.

I love building frameworks and tools that remove boilerplate. Over my career, that's always been my goal: stop the copy-and-paste monotony, the dumb, and build systems that focus on what's valuable to the people using the system. Convincing people to abandoned what they've been taught, got a job doing, feel comfortable and safe in - it doesn't work all that well. Especially when my alternative is something vague and artsy like "creativity".

Suddenly it's 2025/26. And you know what AI can do all day long? Boilerplate.

"Finally", I thought. "We can stop f'ing writing REST controllers." Maybe not the way I imagined we'd stop writing them, since they're still being written. But at least humans can focus on being creative.

## Engineer vs Developer

AI can write entire systems from a few prompts. Code is no longer the valuable thing. What I realized when I started using AI to write code is the value I bring is _engineering_, not _code_. How I was writing with code was valuable: thinking in terms of abstractions, then encoding real principles in those abstractions. Observability, stability, user experience, testability, communication. 

And that changed my perspective on the fear of being the old curmudgeon yelling at the kids to get off my lawn. I _liked_ that AI forced my industry to start caring about engineering over the thoughtless boilerplate repetition.

## Creativity

Now layfolks have access to writing their own apps. This definitely fed the Bay Area Hype Machine: fire your engineers, anyone can write an app now. Stoked plenty of anxiety and fear: mass layoffs, executives making headlines downsizing. AI replacing fast food cashiers, drivers, software engineers, soldiers. 

What I started to see was a new renaissance: people given the power to _create_. The power I have absolutely enjoyed my entire life. The _fun_ of building systems. It's _lego_.

Don't worry. I see the bad stuff, too.
